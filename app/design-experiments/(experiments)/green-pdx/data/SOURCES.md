# Green PDX — data provenance

`green.json` is generated by `_build.py` (re-run: `python3 _build.py`).
Snapshot taken 2026-09-15. Every source is a City of Portland ArcGIS service,
public, no API key, fetched and counted before anything was drawn.

## Tier key

- **A** — primary. The agency that owns the data, publishing it directly.
- **B** — aggregator republishing government data.
- **C** — community or crowdsourced.
- **D** — could not fetch, could not verify, or provenance unclear.

---

## 1. Parks — Portland Parks & Recreation — Tier A

- **Endpoint:** `https://www.portlandmaps.com/arcgis/rest/services/Public/Parks_Misc/MapServer/2`
- **Cadence:** as maintained by the city
- **API key:** none
- **Verified:** 2026-09-15, returned **318** polygons
- **Fields used:** `NAME` → `name`, `ACRES` → `acres`
- **Coordinate system:** requested `outSR=4326` (WGS84)

Total drawn area is **11,438 acres**, which is about 18 square miles inside a
city of roughly 145. That number surprised me enough to double-check it, and it
holds: Forest Park alone is over 5,000 acres.

## 2. Natural areas — Natural Area Land Inventory — Tier A

- **Endpoint:** `https://www.portlandmaps.com/arcgis/rest/services/Public/Parks_Natural_Area_Land_Inventory/MapServer/0`
- **Cadence:** as maintained by the city
- **API key:** none
- **Verified:** 2026-09-15, returned **575** polygons
- **Fields used:** `Name` → `name`, `Owner` → `owner`, `Manager` → `manager`,
  `Management_Unit` → `unit`

**What this layer is not.** It is a land inventory keyed by parcel, not a
directory of named places. **29 of the 575 parcels have no name at all**, and
several share a name with the park they sit inside. So:

> That count was 25 in the first build, because the script treated a
> whitespace-only name as a name. The city's own query
> (`Name IS NOT NULL AND Name <> ''`) returns 546 named, and a number the
> publisher's portal contradicts is not worth printing. Fixed by stripping
> before testing.

- A nameless parcel is drawn and labelled **"Unnamed natural area"**. It is not
  given the name of whatever it is near, and it is not dropped, because it is
  real land the city manages.
- The popup leads with the managing bureau, which every record does have.
- **Natural areas overlap parks.** A parcel inside Forest Park appears in both
  layers, on purpose, because both statements are true. The two fills are
  deliberately light enough to read as two things where they stack.

## 3. Community gardens — Portland Parks & Recreation — Tier A

- **Endpoint:** `https://www.portlandmaps.com/arcgis/rest/services/Public/Parks_Community_Gardens/MapServer/5`
- **Cadence:** as maintained by the city
- **API key:** none
- **Verified:** 2026-09-15, returned **62** polygons, all inside the bbox
- **Fields used:** `Site_Name` → `label`, `Plotspergarden` → `value`,
  `Acres`, `Status`

**Drawn as points, not polygons.** The median garden is a fraction of an acre,
smaller on screen than the dot marking it, so the polygon would be invisible
while the point can carry the plot count. The point is the **area-weighted
centroid of the real polygon**, computed in `_build.py`, not a bounding-box
middle and not a separately published coordinate.

**A garden with no plot count gets no plot row** in its popup. Printing 0 would
read as "no plots", which is a different claim than "not recorded". Across the
62 city gardens the recorded plots total **2,397**; with the six community-run
gardens in section 3b, the map shows **68** gardens and **2,541** plots.

**This layer is why the map stops at the city line.** The program is run by
Portland Parks & Recreation, so gardens exist in the data only inside Portland.
Drawing the metro would put gardens in the middle of an empty region and imply
that Beaverton and Gresham have none, which the data does not say.

## 3b. Community-run gardens — hand-assembled — Tier C

**The city layer is a roster of the city's own program, not a census of
gardens.** That distinction was invisible until James noticed his own garden
missing: Brooklyn Community Garden, at SE Franklin and McLoughlin, sits on ODOT
land and is run by the Brooklyn Action Corps with Southeast Uplift, so Portland
Parks has no record of it. A person looking for their nearest garden does not
care which bureau runs it, so the map now carries both.

`extra-gardens.json` holds **6** records, assembled by hand on 2026-09-16.

| Garden | Run by | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Brooklyn | Brooklyn Action Corps, on ODOT land | brooklyn-neighborhood.org |
| Neighborhoods (SE 162nd) | Outgrowing Hunger | outgrowinghunger.org |
| 139th Ave | Outgrowing Hunger | outgrowinghunger.org |
| Harrison Park | Outgrowing Hunger | outgrowinghunger.org |
| NE 33rd Community Farm | Mudbone Grown, on Oregon Food Bank land | growportland.org |
| Emerson Street | Groundwork Portland | groundworkportland.org |

**The bar for inclusion:** the garden's own operator, or the organization that
manages it, says on its own site that it exists. A third-party list is a lead,
not a source.

**Five candidates failed that bar and are not drawn.** They are kept in
`extra-gardens.json` under `_rejected` so nobody researches them twice:
Cully, Boise–Eliot and Madison (Wikipedia's list, contradicted by the city's own
public page), Market Street (absent from the site of the organization said to
manage it), and Wood (described as city-managed yet missing from the city's
layer, the same contradiction as Cully).

**Locations are approximate.** These are intersections, addresses, or landmarks,
not garden boundaries, so each is good to roughly a block. The popup says so on
every one. Do not treat them as equivalent to the city's surveyed polygons. One
correction already: NE 33rd Community Farm was first placed from the NE 33rd and
Columbia Blvd intersection, about half a kilometre from the Oregon Food Bank
site it actually occupies.

**Nothing here updates itself.** Small gardens open and close, and unlike the
ArcGIS layers this file is only as current as its last hand-check.

**A wobble worth recording:** the city's own page says "There are 60 PP&R
Community Gardens" while its GIS layer returns 62. We draw what the layer
contains and note the disagreement rather than reconciling it silently.

## Transformations

1. **Geometry generalized server-side** with `maxAllowableOffset=0.00012` degrees,
   about 10m, which is finer than one screen pixel at the zoom this map opens
   on. This cuts the payload to 0.54 MB. Shapes are therefore simplified, not
   survey-accurate, and should not be used to locate a property line.
2. **Paging.** ArcGIS caps responses at 2,000 records and reports
   `exceededTransferLimit` rather than an error, so the build script pages. A
   layer that silently ships truncated looks exactly like a layer that is small.
3. **Garden centroids**, as described above.
4. **Field pruning.** Only the fields the interface uses are kept.

## Real vs illustrative

**All 1,086 drawn records are real**, in two tiers of provenance:

- **1,080 Tier A city records** — 318 parks, 575 natural areas, 62 gardens, 125
  neighborhoods — every one fetched from the publishing agency on 2026-09-15 and
  counted.
- **6 Tier C community records** — gardens the city does not run, assembled by
  hand on 2026-09-16 from each operator's own site, with approximate locations.
  See section 3b. Their markers say so.

Nothing on this map is illustrative, synthesized, interpolated, or modelled. No
value is shown that its source does not carry.

## Could not verify

Every layer **drawn** was fetched and counted. One layer was fetched, counted,
and then deliberately **not** drawn per garden, which is the more useful entry.

### Garden Plots — fetched, counted, not attributed

`Parks_Community_Gardens/MapServer/3` holds **3,026** plot polygons, **166** of
them `Plot_Size = 'ADA'`. Per-garden plot and accessibility counts would have
been the best thing on this map. Two joins were attempted on 2026-09-15 and
both failed.

**1. Spatial join.** Test each plot centroid against each garden boundary.
Result: **98 of 3,026 plots** fall inside any garden, about 3%. Ruled out as an
artifact of our own processing:

- Not generalization: fetching both layers at full resolution moves the match
  from 93 to 98, and total garden area barely changes (1.250e-05 → 1.316e-05
  deg²).
- Not paging: all 3,026 plots arrive, verified page by page.
- Not projection: both layers publish EPSG:3857 and identical extents.

The plot polygons simply sit *beside* their garden's boundary rather than
inside it. Colonel Summers Community Garden records 103 plots; 2 land inside
its boundary.

**2. Attribute join.** `ActiveNet_ID` looks like a garden key ("MTTCG.002.A" →
`MTTCG`), and there are 64 prefixes against 62 gardens. It does not hold up:

- Distance from a prefix's own plot centroid to the nearest garden ranges from
  **4m to 638m**; five prefixes sit hundreds of metres from any garden.
- Three gardens are claimed by more than one prefix — Beach by three, including
  the junk keys `Home` and `Patton`. Other non-garden keys exist, such as
  `Bulk Material Deliveries`.
- Where a comparison is possible, counts contradict the city's own
  `Plotspergarden` in both directions: Brentwood South 108 mapped vs 28
  recorded, Adams 76 vs 57, Sewallcrest 90 vs 75.

Assigning each prefix to whichever garden is nearest would be circular, and
"151 of 166 ADA plots landed somewhere" is not the claim a reader would take
from a per-garden number.

**What shipped instead:** the citywide totals, 3,026 mapped plots and 166
ADA-accessible, stated in the page footer as a property of the plot layer
rather than of any garden. Each garden reports only what its own record carries.

**If someone wants this properly:** ask Portland Parks & Recreation which field
joins the two layers, or whether the plot geometry is maintained against a
different base. It is a question for the publisher, not a harder algorithm.

## Color

The palette is the `dataviz` skill's categorical set, chosen by running its
validator against this map's own surface (`#eef2ee`) in all-pairs mode, since a
map shows every category at once.

Two rounds, and both taught something.

**Round one.** The instinctive choice — dark green natural areas, warm orange
gardens — **failed**: orange against `#008300` measures ΔE 3.2 under
protanopia, the classic red-green confusion. It would have looked fine on
screen and been unreadable for roughly one man in twelve. That round shipped
aqua `#1baf7a` parks, green `#008300` natural areas, violet `#4a3aa7` gardens.

**Round two**, from a plain design note: parks should read cooler and more
urban, natural areas drier and yellower. Changing only the natural green to
olive **failed** at ΔE 11.0 normal-vision against the old aqua — two greens a
full-color reader could not reliably separate. Moving both ends apart passed
with the widest margins of anything tried.

**What ships:** park `#4fc3a1`, natural area `#5f7d12`, garden `#4a3aa7`.
Worst all-pairs ΔE 21.0 (deutan) and 21.8 (normal vision), against this map's
own `#eef2ee` surface in all-pairs mode, since a map shows every category at
once.

The lesson worth keeping: **these colors are a set, and a slot cannot be
re-picked alone.** Both failures came from changing one color and leaving its
neighbor.

Park teal sits at 1.92 contrast against this surface, under the 3:1 bar. That
warning is not dismissable, so identity never rests on fill alone: every
polygon is named in its popup, the legend carries a labelled swatch and a count
per layer, gardens print their plot count in the marker, and the two polygon
layers differ in line style as well as hue — parks a solid municipal edge,
natural areas a dashed inventory line. Edge colors (`#1e8f6e`, `#47600d`) are
darker steps of the fills' own hues and are not categorical slots, so they do
not go through the gate.

## Production path

Nothing here needs to become live; parks and gardens move on the order of
years, and the snapshot is committed beside the script that made it. Two real
extensions, in order of appeal:

1. **Per capita.** Metro's RLIS census layers carry `POP` per block group and
   tract with no API key
   (`https://services2.arcgis.com/McQ0OlIABe29rJJy/arcgis/rest/services/Census_Block_Groups_2020/FeatureServer/0`,
   2,155 block groups, verified 2026-09-15). Green acres per 1,000 residents by
   neighborhood is a second view, not a change to this one. Note that the
   Census Bureau's own API now requires a key, and Metro's layers do not.
2. **Widen to the metro** using Metro's ORCA Sites layer (6,462 regional park
   and natural area polygons, verified 2026-09-15). The gardens layer would need
   a caveat drawn in the interface, or a second regional source.
