---
title: "Keep Moving"
subtitle: "On curiosity, momentum, and not needing the whole map"
date: 2026-11-05
author: James Mason
---

Big ideas get heavy when we try to hold the whole thing at once. We picture every step, every person we'd need to convince, every place it could stall, and the idea gets set down for later. The lighter version is just the next question.

A month or two after moving in near McLoughlin Boulevard, I started wondering why it ran at 45 mph. It passes homes, schools, and parks, with Oaks Bottom and the Willamette right alongside, and I can hear it from my house. Slowing down a bit wouldn't really cost anyone their commute, and it would give everyone living along it a little more peace.

It seemed unsafe, so I asked the people whose job is safety. Portland's Vision Zero team was happy to talk, and soon I was emailing PBOT and ODOT too. ODOT's customer service gave themselves 48 hours to answer and cc'd me while they asked around internally. The road's speed hadn't had an engineering review in over 50 years.

Then came my favorite part. The city told me the state had to do a field evaluation. The state told me the city had to request it first. McLoughlin is a state highway running through city limits, so both were right, and for a minute it was a very polite loop with me in the middle.

Vision Zero broke the loop by introducing me to a PBOT engineer. He found a recent speed study that backed a drop to 40 and sent his recommendation to the state within a few weeks. The state said it would take a year. It took a year and a half.

The ODOT engineer leading the investigation listened when I described the families, the schools, and the parks. She ordered another study closer to Milwaukie, and the change grew to reach farther down the corridor than I'd asked for, picking up some signal and sidewalk work along the way. Near the end, with budgets tight everywhere, there might not have been money for the signs. Fortunately, ODOT found another source, and the new signs went up in January 2026.

Then I emailed Waze, Google Maps, and Apple Maps, and asked the city to update its GIS speed data, which feeds some of those apps. If everyone's navigation still says 45, the sign might as well not have changed. Now Waze flashes red at anyone doing 45. Somewhere in there I'd become a project manager without the title.

I never knew the process. I found it one email at a time, and at every step someone was willing to answer the next question. Most of the work was theirs. My part was staying curious and not stopping. It moved slowly, but it never felt stuck.

Most of us are carrying something we imagine differently, whether it's a street, a job, or a whole way of doing things. The full route is rarely visible from the start. The next question usually is.

Five miles per hour doesn't sound like much, but I swear the corridor is quieter now. I think the birds in Oaks Bottom agree. Small, steady progress keeps things moving, and it's usually plenty.
